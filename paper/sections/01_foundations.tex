\section{Foundations and Scope}
\label{sec:foundations}

\subsection{Introduction}
Physical AI concerns agents whose decisions change a physical environment. For a robot, choosing an action requires more than identifying a goal: the system must also contend with what is currently observable, how its body can move, and how objects may respond. A world model makes some of these consequences available as predictions. The central question for this survey is how such predictions contribute to learning and acting under the constraints of physical interaction.

Predictive models can support control through different computational routes. In \emph{World Models}, learned visual and temporal representations support policy learning, including training inside a learned environment~\cite{ha2018worldmodels}. PlaNet instead learns latent dynamics from images and uses online planning to select actions~\cite{hafner2019planet}. These examples motivate a distinction between using prediction to construct a policy and querying predictions while choosing an action. Their experimental settings establish particular uses of learned dynamics rather than a general guarantee of real-world competence.

Robot policies offer a complementary interface: they map observations and instructions to actions. OpenVLA, for example, adapts a vision-language backbone using robot demonstrations to produce visuomotor behavior~\cite{kim2024openvla}. This interface does not by itself expose a future-state predictor that can be queried under alternative actions. The distinction is functional: a policy may contain useful knowledge of dynamics, while an explicit prediction interface provides an additional object that can be inspected, optimized against, or compared with subsequent observations.

The relationship between representation learning and action-conditioned prediction also deserves separate treatment. V-JEPA~2 combines action-free video pretraining with a subsequent action-conditioned model, V-JEPA~2-AC, for robotic planning~\cite{assran2025vjepa2}. The stages serve different purposes and should not be assigned the same control interpretation solely because they share a representation. This motivates recording both the supervision that creates a representation and the interface through which a controller later uses it.

We organize the survey around three questions: \emph{what is predicted, how actions enter the model, and where prediction is used}. These are the survey's comparison axes. Together they connect data and model design to operational roles without requiring all systems to share one architecture or output space. A video generator, a latent predictor, and a structured dynamics model may address different parts of this design space; their comparison should identify the relevant state variables, time horizon, and control interface before assessing performance.

The same role-based approach guides evaluation. Evidence that a model predicts observations accurately supports a different claim from evidence that its predictions improve action selection. Likewise, a closed-loop task result must be interpreted with its task distribution, controller, inference budget, and failure conditions. Our synthesis therefore records predictive performance, downstream use, and deployment conditions separately. This is an analytical choice of the survey, not a universal ranking of papers or a requirement that every supporting component demonstrate a complete robot system.

The article follows four parts. Section~\ref{sec:foundations} establishes the scope and comparison axes. Section~\ref{sec:data} examines the data, representations, and modeling choices that determine which consequences can be learned. Section~\ref{sec:policy} studies how prediction interacts with policies, planning, memory, and recovery. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} organizes evaluation, reliability, safety, and open problems around the claims that a deployed system must substantiate. A companion literature catalog retains seven retrieval categories and exposes source metadata for updating and auditing the evidence base.

\subsection{Working Definitions and Inclusion Boundaries}
We use \emph{world model} for a learned model that predicts aspects of an environment's evolution from available information. For an action-conditioned world model, the inputs additionally specify actions or other interventions. The predicted variables may be observations, latent states, object relations, physical quantities, or task-relevant consequences. This working definition accommodates models learned from passive observations as well as interaction data; it does not assume that every predictive representation already provides an executable robot-action interface.

For analysis, let a history summarize the agent's observations and past actions. A policy maps this information and a goal to an action or action sequence. An action-conditioned world model maps the history and a proposed action sequence to predicted consequences. A controller turns the selected command into realized motion. Separating these interfaces lets us ask whether a failure originates in prediction, action selection, or execution, even when some interfaces are implemented inside a shared network.

The literature corpus includes both direct methods and supporting work. Learned simulators, predictive models used for planning, and prediction-guided policy learning provide direct cases. Datasets, analytical simulators, representation encoders, sensors, and policy baselines provide supporting context according to their demonstrated roles. Their inclusion does not imply that each item is itself a learned world model. In particular, the presence of a reasoning model in a control loop is insufficient to identify an explicit future-prediction mechanism.

\subsection{Review Protocol and Evidence Handling}
This article uses a structured narrative review with a critical evidence map; it does not claim database-complete systematic recall. The base corpus was frozen on 1 October 2026 from 929 records in seven topic categories. Fifteen rows are non-publication gap placeholders; removing them leaves 914 publication candidates and 812 normalized candidate titles. Exact identifiers, normalized titles, and logged manual decisions currently resolve these records into 758 provisional publication families. Cross-category records are retained for navigation and are not counted as independent papers. Three public world-model bibliographies were then frozen by repository commit and compared by arXiv identifier, normalized title, and manually reviewed near-title matches. Targeted searches amend this base when manuscript claims expose a missing mechanism, domain, or evaluation endpoint.

Screening separates direct world-model evidence from supporting resources and boundary comparators. A direct item predicts environment evolution or action consequences and exposes where that prediction enters learning or decision making. Datasets, policies, simulators, safety mechanisms, and reasoning systems are included only for a stated supporting role; they are not relabeled as world models. Formal publications are preferred when available, while recent preprints are retained with their version and publication status. Project pages and code repositories identify resources but do not count as independent experimental validation.

For paper-level verification, we record publication identity, predicted quantity, action interface, system role, experimental setting, denominator, result, author conclusion, claim supported by this review, limitations, and source location. Ten focused evidence batches currently contain 45 such records; the manuscript cites 76 sources overall. A companion search log records exact queries where contemporaneous notes exist and marks unrecorded queries or yields as NR rather than reconstructing them retrospectively. This provides traceability for bounded, source-specific claims but not a PRISMA-style completeness guarantee: formal-version resolution, unified database searches, exclusion counts, and forward and backward citation-search logs remain incomplete.

Figure~\ref{fig:claim-trace} turns this protocol into a claim-to-evidence trace. Its columns are questions rather than a maturity ladder: the evaluation setting changes what can be concluded, while attribution still depends on matched data, computation, candidate actions, controllers, and reporting denominators. A physical success rate can support a system-level observation without identifying which predictive component caused it.

\subsection{From Learned Dynamics to Predictive Robot Systems}
World-model research comprises interacting directions rather than a single replacement sequence. One direction learns dynamics for online decisions. PETS uses probabilistic ensembles and trajectory sampling to propagate uncertainty when evaluating actions~\cite{chua2018pets}; PlaNet brings planning into a learned latent space~\cite{hafner2019planet}. Another direction uses predicted experience to train a policy. Dreamer optimizes behavior through imagined latent trajectories~\cite{hafner2020dreamer}, whereas MBPO augments policy learning with short model rollouts initialized from collected experience~\cite{janner2019mbpo}. The distinction concerns when the model is queried, not whether the final policy is a neural network.

Physical deployment adds sensing, actuation, and interaction costs to this design space. DayDreamer applies world-model learning directly to physical platforms for manipulation, locomotion, and navigation~\cite{wu2023daydreamer}. Foundation-model approaches enter from another direction: they acquire broad visual or semantic priors before adaptation to an action interface. Recent systems such as DreamZero couple future video with action generation~\cite{ye2026dreamzero}. These developments make it useful to retain both the classical model-use distinction and the newer questions of pretraining, embodiment, and execution.

With observation--action history $h_t$, goal $g$, and candidate action sequence $a_{t:t+H-1}$, a predictive model can be written as
\begin{equation}
 p_\theta(y_{t+1:t+H}\mid h_t,a_{t:t+H-1},g),
 \label{eq:predictor}
\end{equation}
where $y$ may represent images, latent features, geometry, or other task-relevant variables. A policy instead produces $\pi_\phi(a_{t:t+H-1}\mid h_t,g)$. These expressions describe interfaces rather than a required architecture. A joint model may implement both, while a model trained on passive video may omit the action argument. The practical question is which interface is trained, queried, and tested.

\subsection{Relation to Recent Surveys and Review Scope}
Recent surveys already cover substantial parts of this landscape. Kirchner et al.~\cite{kirchner2026physicalai} provide a control-centric review of world models for Physical AI, with explicit attention to uncertainty, structural priors, and closed-loop reliability. Hou et al.~\cite{hou2026survey} organize robot world models around policy coupling, learned simulation, and visual predictive modeling. Lu et al.~\cite{lu2026survey} emphasize world-action architectures and control applications. Jena et al.~\cite{jena2026evaluation} focus on evaluation benchmarks and the comparison between prediction and executed behavior. Table~\ref{tab:survey-position} summarizes their stated emphases without interpreting an omitted entry as proof that a topic is absent from a paper.

Topic inclusion is therefore not the differentiator. This review uses the system claim as its comparison unit: what is predicted, how actions condition or consume the prediction, where the model enters learning or execution, and which evaluation setting supports the claim. This cross-layer trace separates direct world-model evidence from supporting resources and boundary comparators, and distinguishes offline prediction, simulated closed loop, and physical closed loop without treating them as a universal quality ranking. The seven collection categories supply literature to four narrative parts: foundations establish interfaces; data and representations determine what can be predicted; integration explains how predictions affect behavior; evaluation examines whether that use is reliable.

The present manuscript therefore makes bounded synthesis claims rather than completeness claims. Its companion protocol records the frozen catalog, provisional publication-family layer, external-library commits, deduplication order, inclusion boundaries, focused search batches, and corpus amendments.
