\section{Integration with Robot Policies}
\label{sec:policy}

\subsection{Planning with Predicted Consequences}
The most direct use of a world model is to compare candidate actions before executing one. PETS uses uncertainty-aware dynamics within trajectory sampling~\cite{chua2018pets}; PlaNet and TD-MPC2 plan in learned latent spaces~\cite{hafner2019planet,hansen2024tdmpc2}. Their predictive models enter an online decision procedure whose components include candidate generation, rollout, outcome scoring, and the feedback that replaces predictions with observations.

A generic receding-horizon decision can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
 a^*_{t:t+H-1}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a_{t:t+H-1}}
 \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\gamma^k r(y_{t+k+1},a_{t+k})\right],
 \label{eq:planning}
\end{equation}
with an initial action or chunk executed before replanning. Here $r$ scores predicted outcomes and $\gamma$ discounts future reward. This is a decision template, not an assertion that all cited systems implement the same optimizer. Learned values, terminal costs, constraints, and uncertainty penalties can change the objective. A better planner, evaluator, or candidate generator can also improve task success independently of the predictor.

Rollout length trades anticipation of delayed effects against reliance on increasingly uncertain predictions. Frequent feedback limits open-loop execution but cannot make an inaccurate model harmless: the first selected action can itself place a robot in a difficult state. Horizon, sampling budget, and control rate therefore belong in a method comparison alongside architecture. A planning result is most interpretable when predictive quality and action-selection behavior are examined under the same task conditions.

An action-conditioned JEPA study adds a training-time state-alignment objective to inverse dynamics, regressing consecutive latent pairs onto the benchmark's measured configuration and motion variables~\cite{liu2026physicallygroundedjepa}. At deployment, it rolls candidate action sequences through the latent predictor and selects them with CEM. Across four simulated goal-conditioned tasks, state alignment improves success over the paper's IDM-only ablation on all four; the method reports 100\% on TwoRoom, 85\% on Reacher, 98\% on PushT, and 87\% on OGBench-Cube, averaged over three seeds and 50 fixed start--goal problems per seed. This is direct evidence for action-conditioned latent planning with privileged state supervision in these benchmarks. The comparisons to DINO-WM, PLDM, and LeWorldModel use values reported by LeWorldModel rather than a common re-evaluation, and the study includes no physical-robot trials or direct predictive-error comparison. Its results therefore support the state-alignment ablation within this simulator protocol, not physical deployment or a general advantage over other JEPA objectives.

GeoWorld shows why planning also requires a domain qualifier~\cite{zhang2026geoworld}. It combines a hyperbolic predictive representation with energy-based CEM to select procedural action-label sequences between observed and goal clips. In the matched ViT-g384 video setting, its success rate exceeds V-JEPA~2 by 1.55--2.55 percentage points across the reported CrossTask and COIN horizons, while image-based gains are less uniform. These metrics compare predicted instructional-video steps with annotated sequences; they do not execute motor commands or test physical consequences. The result supports geometry-aware procedural planning on these datasets, not embodied control.

\subsection{Driving and Navigation as an Interface Test}
Driving studies make the model--decision interface unusually explicit. Drive-WM conditions multiview video prediction on candidate ego trajectories and scores the generated futures with map and object rewards~\cite{wang2024drivewm}. In its nuScenes offline evaluation, selecting among three high-level commands reduces average trajectory error and collision rate relative to a random-command condition, approaching a planner given the ground-truth command. The evidence supports candidate ranking under logged-scene metrics; it is not an interactive road deployment or a general safety guarantee.

Occupancy models expose a related distinction. OccWorld jointly generates future 3D occupancy and ego trajectory tokens~\cite{zheng2024occworld}; the trajectory is a model output rather than a candidate whose consequences are compared. Drive-OccWorld instead conditions future occupancy on trajectory proposals and scores them with agent, road, and learned-volume costs~\cite{yang2025driveoccworld}. Its cost ablations connect structured predictions to trajectory selection, but evaluation remains open-loop on nuScenes and does not remove the complete predictive module while holding the planner fixed. Joint prediction and counterfactual planning should therefore remain separate categories.

Prediction can also influence a policy only during training. DriveVLA-W0 adds autoregressive or diffusion future-image objectives to a driving VLA, yet bypasses both visual-generation paths during driving inference~\cite{li2026drivevlaw0}. Its action-only comparisons and data-scale study support dense predictive supervision as a representation-learning mechanism, especially at the largest reported scale. The 70-million-frame dataset is proprietary, some smaller-scale metrics do not improve monotonically, and NAVSIM is a predictive-driver-model benchmark rather than a physical closed loop.

DriveDreamer-Policy similarly trains depth, future-video, and trajectory heads through ordered query groups, but its action head can run without explicitly generating depth or video at deployment~\cite{zhou2026drivedreamerpolicy}. On NAVSIM v1, adding depth or video supervision to the action-only model changes PDMS from 88.0 to 88.5 or 88.9, and using both yields 89.2; the complete model reports 88.7 EPDMS on v2. This matched ablation supports complementary predictive supervision inside this architecture. It does not establish online look-ahead or physical driving: NAVSIM evaluates logged scenes with a predictive driver model, the depth targets and depth metric both use DA3 pseudo-depth, and the ablation reports neither variance nor significance.

ADriver-I provides an earlier modular version of recurrent world--action generation~\cite{jia2023adriveri}. It predicts speed and steering from interleaved visual--action history, generates the next frames conditioned on that action, and feeds generated frames into later predictions. Its control and video metrics are evaluated separately on nuScenes and a private highway dataset; the recurrent ``driving'' result is qualitative and occurs only in self-generated video. It therefore establishes an interface precedent, not evidence that imagined futures improve closed-loop driving.

DriveWAM makes the generated future an explicit action input~\cite{shi2026drivewam}. Each inference chunk first samples a future video latent and then decodes an action chunk from that latent; new observations re-anchor the next step, while selective video--action KV memory bounds history. On a curated 1,000-clip subset of PhysicalAI-AV, the reported 4-second ADE/FDE is 0.83/2.47. Selective memory retains 0.89/2.52 while reducing the reported 300-second cache from 3.07 to 0.25 GB relative to full KV memory. These are offline trajectory and systems measurements: the test subset is author-curated, external models use different training data, and the ablation does not isolate generated-future conditioning from the complete pretrained and jointly supervised architecture.

DAWN instead alternates action denoising with an action-conditioned short latent rollout~\cite{lu2026dawn}. In its matched NAVSIM v1 ablation, adding a predictor changes PDMS from 82.8 to 85.2 and enabling bidirectional world--action refinement raises it to 87.9. A 2-second latent rollout reaches 87.3 versus 87.9 at 4 seconds, while reported latency rises from 331 ms without rollout to 1,068 ms at 4 seconds. This supports short reciprocal inference within the tested model, but not deployment safety: NAVSIM and nuScenes are logged-scene protocols, no real vehicle is evaluated, and DAWN's NAVSIM v2 aggregate score is below several listed baselines.

Navigation introduces language-conditioned long horizons. The NavForesee preprint jointly predicts short-horizon geometry, long-horizon milestones, and waypoints under a hierarchical language plan~\cite{liu2026navforesee}. Removing either prediction horizon degrades its R2R-CE simulator results, while removing the language planner causes a larger drop. This supports complementarity within that system, not attribution of the whole gain to prediction. Its RxR-CE result is less uniformly favorable, and the current evidence is simulated and preprint-only.

Visual goal navigation provides a more direct prediction--selection interface. Navigation World Models (NWM) predicts future views under continuous actions and uses CEM either to construct a trajectory or to rerank samples from an external policy~\cite{bar2025nwm}. Its RECON results establish that generated futures can enter an offline trajectory objective, but they are not physical closed-loop trials; the paper also reports context loss in unfamiliar scenes and difficulty modeling pedestrians. DreamerNav instead learns a DreamerV3 policy from latent dynamics while following a static global A* path~\cite{shanks2025dreamernav}. Its main comparisons use one simulated warehouse, followed by preliminary demonstrations on two quadruped platforms without a reported physical-trial denominator. These works therefore connect prediction to action selection and deployment, respectively, without jointly establishing robust real-world replanning.

Recent preprints move closer to a measured feedback loop. NavThinker queries action-conditioned next-state features and predicted pedestrian trajectories inside a social-navigation policy~\cite{hu2026navthinker}. Its Social-HM3D ablation improves success when look-ahead features are added, but its Unitree Go2 result is a qualitative scenario without repeated-trial statistics. NavWAM jointly predicts action chunks, future views, states, and goal-progress values, then executes direct action chunks and re-queries the model after new observations~\cite{azuma2026navwam}. In 24 image-goal episodes per method across four indoor environments, the preprint reports 19 successes for NavWAM, 14 for OmniVLA, and 4 for NWM. This is closed-loop physical evidence within one small protocol. It does not isolate future-view prediction in the physical comparison: future views and values accompany the default policy output but are not required for execution, while the predictive-head ablations are offline.

\subsection{Policy Learning with Imagined Experience}
A world model can influence behavior without conducting a full online search at every control step. Dreamer learns an actor through imagined latent trajectories~\cite{hafner2020dreamer}. MBPO uses short synthetic rollouts branched from real experience for policy optimization~\cite{janner2019mbpo}. These approaches move some predictive computation into learning. They differ from online planning in deployment cost, interaction requirements, and where model errors affect behavior.

DayDreamer anchors this discussion in physical sensing and actuation~\cite{wu2023daydreamer}. More generally, imagined learning involves two coupled feedback loops: interaction updates the model, and the model changes the policy that collects subsequent interaction. Analysis should record both loops, including the reward source and whether policy-induced distribution changes are reflected in model updates.

World-VLA-Loop makes the second loop explicit~\cite{liu2026worldvlaloop}. It trains an action-conditioned video model with successful and near-success trajectories, predicts both future frames and rewards, performs VLA reinforcement learning inside the model, and then uses rollouts from the updated physical policy to refine the next model. The reported two-task robot study improves after two iterations, but still requires task-specific demonstrations and new physical rollouts. The result therefore supports co-adaptation under policy shift, not elimination of real interaction.

VLAW provides a formally published physical example of the same broad co-improvement pattern~\cite{guo2026vlaw}. On one DROID platform, it starts from 25 expert demonstrations per task category, collects 50 physical rollouts per category in each of two rounds, adapts an action-conditioned video model, and generates 500 synthetic trajectories per task for policy updates. Across five tasks, the reported mean success is 86.8\% after round two, compared with 75.2\% for filtered behavior cloning using the same physical rollout budget; each of these evaluations uses 50 trials per task. This 11.6-point within-paper gap supports supplemental synthetic rollouts under the study's reward filtering and task adaptation, not replacement of real interaction or cross-platform generalization.

WorldSample keeps physical interaction in the loop while expanding each real rollout with counterfactual world-model transitions~\cite{xue2026worldsample}. Q-aware selection and uncertainty-guided scheduling determine which synthetic transitions enter online RL. On five tasks using one Galaxea arm, its table reports average success of 82\% versus 56\% for HIL-SERL, with 23K versus 56K online steps. On the insertion task, removing scheduling changes success from 95\% to 61\% and intervention rate from 24\% to 43\%. The preprint does not report the denominator behind each success estimate, and the complete method also changes reward labeling, sample selection, scheduling, and model adaptation. It therefore supports selective real--synthetic online learning in a fixed task distribution, not a model-only causal claim.

WHIRL uses human intervention as a predictive training target rather than only a corrective action~\cite{yin2026whirl}. During physical HIL-RL, a latent world model predicts next-state reward, termination, dynamics, uncertainty, and the probability that the operator would take over; only the last term supplies actor-side risk shaping. On five Franka--LEAP tasks, the full system improves held-out autonomous success by 15--30 percentage points over its matched no-world-model HIL residual-RL baseline, while the largest reported rolling step-weighted intervention reduction is from 0.113 to 0.018 on Pull Drawer. The result is bounded by one seed and one operator per task--method cell, in-region resets, and one embodiment. It supports learning a forward risk signal from interventions, not cross-operator calibration or a runtime alarm that autonomously executes recovery.

Hi-WM moves a different human loop into the learned environment~\cite{li2026hiwm}. An operator interrupts failure-prone model rollouts, rewinds cached states, and supplies multiple corrective branches for post-training. Across three physical tasks and two policy backbones, the preprint reports a 19.0-point average gain over a world-model closed-loop baseline under matched post-training budgets. However, it omits the physical evaluation trial count and relies on human choices inside the simulator. The intervention is training data collection in a world model, not an autonomous alarm or physical runtime takeover.

Synthetic volume is not a sufficient measure of training value. MBPO's restricted rollouts illustrate that model use can be selective rather than maximized~\cite{janner2019mbpo}. For foundation-scale systems, the corresponding comparison should account for generating, filtering, and training on synthetic trajectories. Real interaction, synthetic interaction, and computation remain separate resources; a reduction in one is not automatically a reduction in all three.

\subsection{Memory, Failure Detection, and Recovery}
Long-horizon prediction and recovery depend on different uses of history. Memory can preserve hidden scene state or task progress; a failure detector decides that execution has departed from an expected state; a recovery mechanism selects corrective behavior. These functions may share representations, but evidence for one does not establish the others.

Mem-World uses an action-conditioned, wrist-view-centered 4D memory to retrieve geometrically relevant observations during video rollout~\cite{zheng2026memworld}. Across five manipulation tasks, its predicted policy success rates correlate more strongly with physical success than those of its Ctrl-World comparator ($r=0.97$ versus $0.85$). Synthetic post-training raises average physical success from 58\% to 72\% on three long-horizon tasks, but the data pipeline retains only 20--30 successful rollouts per task from 200 candidates using human annotation. The result supports persistent simulation and data generation under that protocol; it is not an online recovery test, and the model can still predict physically invalid grasps.

WorldScape Policy~2.0 instead combines recent visual context with event-level history intended to track completed subgoals~\cite{su2026worldscape2}. In its clean-only RoboTwin ablation, success averaged across clean and randomized tests rises from 40.91\% without memory to 44.67\% with short-term memory, 46.25\% after adding long-term event memory, and 47.89\% after adding latent subgoal reasoning. The preprint also reports 20 physical trials per task on one dual-arm platform. Those physical comparisons vary the complete system rather than memory alone and do not inject predefined failures, so they support progress-aware execution more directly than failure recovery.

Failure-Aware RL supplies a prediction--intervention interface~\cite{li2026farl}. A short-horizon latent world model predicts future constraint violations and switches from the task policy to an offline-trained recovery policy when risk exceeds a threshold. The authors compare FARL with an offline-to-online baseline over 50 training episodes on each of three Franka tasks and report fewer failure state--action pairs. This is physical evidence for anticipatory action replacement during post-training, but the mechanism usually prevents a predicted failure rather than restoring the task after failure; it also uses task, recovery, and failure demonstrations collected for each task.

Dream2Fix uses a world model at a different point: recovery-data construction rather than runtime planning~\cite{li2026dream2fix}. It perturbs successful real demonstrations inside Ctrl-World, verifies the generated failures, and trains a VLM to output structured trajectory corrections. On one Franka setup, 50 trials per setting yield task-level recovery rates of 46\% on benchmark failures and 40\% on OpenVLA deployment failures, compared with 36\% and 30\% for Gemini-3-Flash. Its often-quoted 81.3\% result is instead offline correction-label accuracy on the generated test set. The physical evidence therefore shows partial closed-loop recovery for four controlled grasp-related failure types, not general state restoration; the world model generates training data but is not the deployed recovery policy.

Post-failure correction need not use a predictive dynamics model. ViFailback monitors a VLA with an external vision-language model and returns textual or visual-symbol guidance after detecting a failure~\cite{zeng2026vifailback}. On three previously unseen physical tasks with 21 trials per task and matched initial layouts, its two correction interfaces raise average success from 50.8--52.4\% to 73.0--74.6\%. This formal CVPR study supports failure diagnosis and corrective supervision, not a world-model claim. Together, these examples motivate reporting memory, prevention, detection, correction, state restoration, and task resumption as separate capabilities.

\subsection{Visual Plans and Joint World--Action Models}
A generated future can serve as an intermediate action representation. UniPi synthesizes goal-conditioned video and extracts actions from it~\cite{du2023unipi}. This separates choosing what should happen from translating that outcome into commands. It also identifies a failure boundary: a plausible plan can be unreachable by the current robot, and a correct plan can be poorly translated into motor actions. Plan quality and action grounding require separate examination.

Joint world--action models reduce this separation by learning future observations and commands together. DreamZero builds on a pretrained video diffusion model to generate video and actions~\cite{ye2026dreamzero}. Cosmos Policy encodes actions, future observations, and values within a video model's latent generation process, and examines direct-policy and planning modes~\cite{kim2026cosmospolicy}. These examples illustrate the interface convergence discussed in recent surveys~\cite{hou2026survey,lu2026survey}, without implying that every video-based policy performs an explicit future rollout at execution time.

Motus broadens this joint interface into switchable understanding, video-generation, inverse-dynamics, action, and video--action modes~\cite{bi2026motus}. On 50 RoboTwin tasks evaluated with 100 executions per task, it reports 88.66\% and 87.02\% average success in clean and randomized scenes; removing the video-generation expert in a smaller matched simulation ablation reduces success from 77.00\% to 25.50\%. Its two-platform physical study instead reports partial subtask completion and omits the evaluation denominator. The evidence supports joint pretraining and a useful video expert in the tested simulation, plus physical feasibility, but not a general performance ranking or explicit online planning.

JOPAT changes which future variables are coupled to action generation~\cite{guan2026jopat}. It jointly denoises actions, visual latents, 2D point tracks, and visibility, then executes action chunks in a receding-horizon loop. With data and optimization held fixed on LIBERO-Long, the joint model reports 96.4\% average success versus 77.4\% for latent-only prediction. Across four LeRobot SO-101 tasks with ten rollouts per method and task, the same comparison is 57.5\% versus 35.0\%. These matched ablations support point correspondences as a complementary control interface within JOPAT. They do not establish broad superiority: the physical sample is small, precision insertion remains at 1/10, and track supervision inherits errors from an off-the-shelf tracker.

VAMPO instead changes the training objective of the predictive branch~\cite{ge2026vampo}. It post-trains a video prediction model with GRPO and an expert-future latent reward before training the action-generation module. On CALVIN ABC$\rightarrow$D, the base average completed-sequence length is 4.28; post-training only the predictor while retaining the original action module gives 4.35, and retraining the action module on the revised representation gives 4.56. The decomposition supports objective alignment as a viable post-training route, while also showing that the larger gain follows downstream adaptation rather than predictor replacement alone. The preprint reports no variance, its checkpoints fluctuate from 4.31 to 4.56, and its physical figure omits evaluation denominators, so it does not yet establish a general causal link from latent accuracy to physical control.

V-JEPA Policy provides a matched test of future-latent supervision inside a policy~\cite{zhang2026vjepapolicy}. A frozen V-JEPA~2.1 encoder defines the visual space; a future predictor supplies layer-wise context states to a flow-matching action expert, but does not condition its predictions on candidate actions. With data, architecture, training seed, and update budget fixed, removing the future loss while retaining future queries changes success from 97.25\% to 91.65\% on LIBERO and from 79.25\% to 68.81\% on LIBERO-Plus. Predictor-only pretraining on DROID, followed by matched downstream training, changes the two physical task results from 11/20 and 7/20 to 15/20 and 17/20. These comparisons support explicit predictive supervision and transferable predictor initialization within this architecture, not explicit model-based search or a cross-paper ranking: the pretraining adds 100,000 upstream updates, the ablation uses one training seed, and the physical test covers one dual-arm platform and two tasks.

Two neighboring JEPA-style systems place prediction earlier in the pipeline. VLA-JEPA learns latent transition tokens from current observations using future V-JEPA targets, then adapts a flow-matching action head~\cite{sun2026vlajepa}. In its within-paper human-video ablation, LIBERO-Plus changes from 62.9\% to 79.5\%, but LIBERO changes only from 96.1\% to 97.2\% and the paper reports no benefit on one SimplerEnv robot average. JEPA-WAM instead uses a joint current--future target to shape a shared policy predictor and removes the transition branch at deployment~\cite{lin2026jepawam}. Under one matched LIBERO-Plus recipe, disabling transition prediction while retaining V-JEPA features scores 77.0\%, versus 79.2\% for the full design. Its five-task physical study averages normalized completion over 10 rollouts per task and setting, rather than binary success. These results support training-time predictive representation shaping within the reported systems; neither method supplies candidate-action-conditioned online rollout, and neither reports multi-seed training uncertainty.

JEPA-VLA moves prediction farther upstream: it feeds frozen V-JEPA~2 embeddings from the two most recent frames into an otherwise direct policy, without a downstream future target or transition predictor~\cite{miao2026jepavla}. In the authors' OpenVLA-OFT implementation, adding the encoder and gated fusion changes average LIBERO success from 90.30\% to 96.40\%, and the six-task RoboTwin easy/hard averages from 54.8/9.3\% to 73.5/17.7\%. This supports the complete representation-fusion package, not the predictive objective alone: encoder capacity, fusion layers, and compute all change, no multi-seed uncertainty is reported, and the physical study covers one task with an incompletely specified per-condition denominator. JEPA-VLA is therefore evidence for predictive pretraining as a visual prior, not for a downstream world model or online imagination.

NoiseGate treats the reliability of each imagined video latent as another control variable inside joint denoising~\cite{huang2026noisegate}. It freezes the video--action backbone and uses simulator reward to train a per-latent timestep scheduler. In its 50-task RoboTwin configuration, average success changes from 92.58\% for the demonstration-trained backbone to 94.28\% after scheduler training. A separate 15-task, smaller-backbone ablation reports 57.5\% for shared timesteps, 61.3\% after independent-noise training, 63.4\% with a hand-crafted schedule, and 67.5\% with learned scheduling. The protocols must remain separate: the result is simulation-only, uses a single reported seed, and spends additional online rollout interaction to optimize the scheduler.

WALL-WM changes the temporal unit rather than only the objective or schedule~\cite{li2026wallwm}. It aligns language, predicted video, and action at semantic event boundaries and supports variable-length event execution. On internal real-robot suites, a pretrained event-mode system exceeds a pretrained fixed-length baseline without view-interaction attention in Task Progress, from 32.6 to 71.6 on reasoning manipulation and 22.0 to 53.75 on generalization. This comparison simultaneously changes cross-view attention and execution format, and the paper does not report physical trial counts or uncertainty. Together with acknowledged platform-aligned data and tuning advantages, it supports event-level execution as a promising combined system design, not a single-factor causal claim or a cross-platform result.

WAV combines a future-state model, a trajectory-value model, and action decoding through iterative latent inference~\cite{li2026wav}. Removing its latent planning stage reduces its reported LIBERO average from 98.1 to 96.4, with the largest drop on the Long suite. Its three-task physical comparison also favors WAV over a baseline that shares its video-pretrained model, although each task uses a separate model and only 15 trials. This is evidence for an implicit planning interface under those conditions, rather than a universal advantage over explicit search.

LaRA-VLA marks a useful boundary~\cite{bai2026laravla}. It predicts a next-observation latent from the current image, instruction, and reasoning state, then uses inverse-dynamics supervision and an action expert to produce commands. The predicted future token is not conditioned on candidate actions and is not queried to compare their consequences. On SimplerEnv, its matched ablation raises average success from 55.2\% without chain-of-thought components to 68.8\% with latent textual and visual reasoning; its physical study uses 12 trials for each of four long-horizon task categories. These results support predictive latent supervision inside a policy, but not an action-conditioned forward model or counterfactual planner. We therefore retain LaRA-VLA as a boundary comparator rather than a core world-model instance.

DynBridge and MM-ACT sharpen the same distinction~\cite{wang2026dynbridge,liang2026mmact}. DynBridge trains interaction tokens with future-trajectory reconstruction and action imitation; its five-task Franka study uses 10 trials and compares the full policy with an internal no-dynamics ablation. MM-ACT jointly supervises text, future images, and actions: on eight RoboTwin tasks, adding text and image generation to the action-only setting raises average success from 43.13\% to 52.38\%, while its three-task Franka comparison uses 20 trials per task but does not repeat that component ablation. Both support prediction as policy supervision under their protocols. Neither exposes a candidate-action-conditioned forward model for counterfactual search, and MM-ACT's physical result cannot isolate the predictive objective.

For a joint system, a predicted outcome may be consumed by action selection, shape training through an auxiliary loss, or remain another generated output. The mechanisms can coexist, but suggest different ablations. They should not be collapsed into an undifferentiated statement that a policy ``uses a world model.''

WorldSync addresses whether a learned simulator follows actions outside expert demonstrations~\cite{chen2026worldsync}. Its WorldEcho protocol pairs visual-integrity gating with end-effector trajectory alignment over expert and feasible off-expert queries. In a matched two-round policy-improvement study on one physical cup-stacking task, both policies start at 48\% success; the WorldSync condition reaches 68\%, while an expert-trained CtrlWorld condition reaches 56\%. The paper does not state the physical trial denominator, and its component ablations are performed in RoboTwin rather than on the robot. The evidence connects broader action coverage and intervention-effect supervision to downstream improvement in that protocol, but does not isolate a universal action-faithfulness mechanism.

\subsection{Reasoning-Policy Hybrids: The Astra Case}
Recent Astra evaluations motivate distinguishing direct numerical action generation from a hybrid interface that reviews a learned policy's proposals. In Su et al.'s technical report~\cite{su2026astra}, Astra can correct actions proposed by $\pi_{0.5}$. On ten selected RoboDojo tasks with five aligned cases per task, the authors report 48\% success for the hybrid and 26\% for direct Astra control. The public-policy comparisons use published reference scores rather than same-seed reruns. These findings support a bounded discussion of complementary action-generation and correction roles; they do not isolate a future-prediction mechanism.

A separate evaluation by Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2026unexpected} covers all 42 RoboDojo tasks, with 50 episodes per task for Astra. It reports 22.48\% average success over 2,100 trials and a capability profile that varies substantially across task types. This broader result should be discussed alongside the selected-task hybrid study, while keeping their task sets and denominators separate.

The Galbot team's September 29 preprint~\cite{galbot2026astra} extends the investigation across six domains, including cooperation with learned policies. Its gripper-manipulation discussion includes the same 48\% RoboDojo hybrid result. We treat the overlapping result as part of one evidence series rather than a second independent replication. The paper also documents inference constraints, including paused physics during its locomotion reasoning calls. This makes the relation between simulated success and time-critical control an explicit part of the discussion.

Black Forest Labs' FLUX~3 Action report~\cite{bfl2026flux3action} examines another combination: a fast action policy supplies proposals that Astra may accept, edit, replace, or stop. The report connects hybrid performance to inference cost and elapsed time, alongside task completion. This provides an industrial case for evaluating the entire decision loop, with the report's own protocol and provenance retained.

For world-model research, these interfaces raise a specific attribution question. Does a hybrid improve because it predicts action consequences, identifies the correct target, repairs a proposal, or uses more computation? A useful comparison would hold the base policy, observations, control limits, and inference budget fixed while varying the information supplied to the reviewer: current observations, proposed actions, and explicit predicted consequences. This comparison is a proposed evaluation design of the survey, not an experiment established by the cited reports. It connects the Astra discussion to the article's main question: when, and through which interface, does prediction change action selection?
